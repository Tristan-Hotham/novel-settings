Where novels are set

Wikidata, the free database behind Wikipedia, records the real places where {{source.novels|int}} novels are set, and {{source.other|int}} other plays, stories, poems and books besides. I put them all on one map. Fiction turns out to love three cities, to travel less than you might think, and to leave most of the world almost unwritten. The map also shows where a few volunteers have been very busy.

Every novel happens somewhere. Some of those somewheres are invented, but most are real: Dickens's London, Balzac's Paris, Chandler's Los Angeles. Wikidata records them with a property called narrative location, and because each place carries coordinates, the settings of literature can be put on a map. I downloaded every literary work in Wikidata with at least one real narrative location: {{source.works|int}} works, {{source.novels|int}} of them novels, set in {{source.places|int}} distinct places across {{n_countries|int}} countries.

The map below lets you search for a book or an author, filter by genre and period, and draw a line from where an author was born to where their story takes place. Then scroll on for what the map says about fiction, and about the people who catalogue it. Wikidata is written by volunteers, and in a few places a single dedicated project has added more settings than the rest of the world combined. That is part of the story too.

    02 · The big threeLondon, New York, Paris

    Three cities stand far above the rest. {{spot.London.n|int}} novels are set at least partly in London, {{spot.New York City.n|int}} in New York and {{spot.Paris.n|int}} in Paris. Together they appear in {{city_top3_pct|int}}% of all the novels on the map. Count every literary work, including plays, short stories and poems, and Paris comes first, with {{spot.Paris.all|int}}, ahead of New York's {{spot.New York City.all|int}} and London's {{spot.London.all|int}}: French plays and stories are particularly well recorded. After them come Los Angeles ({{spot.Los Angeles.n|int}} novels), San Francisco ({{spot.San Francisco.n|int}}), Rome ({{spot.Rome.n|int}}) and Madrid ({{spot.Madrid.n|int}}).

    Single authors shape the list. Benito Pérez Galdós, who chronicled nineteenth century Spain, accounts for {{author_facts.Benito Pérez Galdós.top_city_n|int}} of Madrid's novels; Rex Stout's detective Nero Wolfe for {{author_facts.Rex Stout.top_city_n|int}} of New York's; Georges Simenon's Inspector Maigret for {{author_facts.Georges Simenon.top_city_n|int}} of Paris's. And one name in the top ten needs explaining. Pittsburgh is eighth, with {{spot.Pittsburgh.n|int}} novels, ahead of Chicago ({{spot.Chicago.n|int}}) and Boston ({{spot.Boston.n|int}}). Pittsburgh is not the literary capital of America, but the novels set there are unusually well catalogued in Wikidata. Mumbai, a city of about {{spot.Mumbai.pop_m|int}} million people, has {{spot.Mumbai.n|int}}; Lagos {{spot.Lagos.n|int}}; Tokyo {{spot.Tokyo.n|int}}.

    03 · Two centuriesParis, then London, then New York

    The three cities have taken turns. In novels published between 1830 and 1849, the age of Balzac, Hugo and Eugène Sue, Paris is the setting of {{trio20.0.Paris_pct|int}}% of the novels with a mapped place, against {{trio20.0.London_pct|int}}% for London. By the turn of the twentieth century London had caught up, and it led between the wars. After 1950 New York took over: {{trio20.6.New York_pct|int}}% of novels published from 1950 to 1969 are set there, more than in London ({{trio20.6.London_pct|int}}%) or Paris ({{trio20.6.Paris_pct|int}}%). Since 1990 London has edged back in front, while Paris has faded to about {{trio20.8.Paris_pct|int}}%.

    London, Paris and New York, by publication period

    Share of novels with a mapped place (a city, site or area) that are set at least partly in each city, by twenty years of first publication. The early periods are small samples (counts under the axis). Hover a point.

    All three shares fall in the most recent decades because the map has grown wider, not because the cities have lost their appeal: novels published since 1990 are spread across many more places. Wikidata holds far more recent novels than old ones; half of the novels with a date were first published in {{years.median_novel|raw}} or later.

    04 · Home and abroadMost novels stay close to home

    For {{home.n|int}} novels Wikidata knows both where at least one author was born and the country of at least one setting. {{home.home_pct|int}}% of them are set entirely in the country where their author was born (judged by today's borders), and {{home.abroad_pct|int}}% go abroad at least once. The distances are short. Measuring from the author's birthplace to each mapped place, the median is {{home.median_km|int}} km, and {{home.within_50|int}}% of settings lie within 50 km of where the author was born. Writers really do write about home.

    How far from home

    Distance from the author's birthplace to each mapped setting of a novel ({{home.pairs|int}} novel and place pairs; for co-written books the nearest author). Hover a bar.

    Some travel much further. The longest journey on the map belongs to Ngaio Marsh, born in New Zealand, who set {{author_facts.Ngaio Marsh.top_country_n|int}} of her {{author_facts.Ngaio Marsh.novels|int}} mapped detective novels in Britain: {{farthest.0.title|raw}}, set in {{farthest.0.place|raw}}, lies {{farthest.0.km|int}} km from her birthplace in Christchurch, almost the far side of the planet. P. L. Travers, born in Australia, set all {{author_facts.P L Travers.novels|int}} of her Mary Poppins books in London. Among the {{authors_far_n|int}} authors with at least three mapped novels, those two travel furthest.

    Who goes abroad, by the author's country of birth

    Share of novels set at least partly outside the present-day country where the author was born. Countries with at least 40 such novels. Hover a bar for where they go.

    Birthplace is not nationality, and the chart shows it. Writers born in what is now Ukraine top the list ({{home_country.UKR.abroad_pct|int}}% abroad) because Joseph Roth, Irène Némirovsky, Mikhail Bulgakov and the Italian crime writer Giorgio Scerbanenco were all born there and set their books in Austria, France, Russia and Italy. Belgium's {{home_country.BEL.abroad_pct|int}}% is mostly Simenon, born in Liège, whose Maigret lives in Paris. Nigeria's {{home_country.NGA.abroad_pct|int}}% owes much to Simon Scarrow, born in Lagos, whose {{author_facts.Simon Scarrow.novels|int}} Roman army novels are mostly recorded as set in Italy. At the other end, {{home_country.USA.abroad_pct|int}}% of novels by American-born writers go abroad, and {{home_country.GBR.abroad_pct|int}}% of those by British-born writers.

    05 · GenresCrime goes to Paris, science fiction to San Francisco

    Wikidata gives a genre for many novels. Crime and mystery is the biggest group with real settings, {{genres.Crime and mystery.n|int}} novels, and its capital is Paris ({{crime_top.0.1|int}}, many of them Maigret's), then London ({{crime_top.1.1|int}}) and New York ({{crime_top.2.1|int}}). Relative to all novels, crime is unusually drawn to Miami, Oslo, Edinburgh and Venice, home of Donna Leon's Commissario Brunetti. Science fiction that uses real places goes to New York and London, but it is {{genres.Science fiction.lift.0.2|f1}} times as likely as the average novel to be set in San Francisco. Fantasy with a real setting belongs above all to London ({{genres.Fantasy.cities.0.1|int}}). Historical novels lean towards Europe: Paris ({{genres.Historical.cities.0.1|int}}), London ({{genres.Historical.cities.1.1|int}}) and Rome ({{genres.Historical.cities.3.1|int}}). And {{offworld.moon_works|int}} works in Wikidata are set at least partly on the Moon, and {{offworld.mars_works|int}} on Mars; they are counted here but not mapped.

    06 · The unwritten worldEurope and North America, then a long way down

    Novels on the map are concentrated in two continents. {{continents.Europe.novels_pct|int}}% of novels with a setting in a known country are set at least partly in Europe, which is home to {{continents.Europe.pop_pct|int}}% of the world's people; {{continents.North America.novels_pct|int}}% in North America, home to {{continents.North America.pop_pct|int}}%. Asia, with {{continents.Asia.pop_pct|int}}% of humanity, has {{continents.Asia.novels_pct|int}}% of the novels, and Africa, with {{continents.Africa.pop_pct|int}}%, has {{continents.Africa.novels_pct|int}}%. Per person, Europe has {{cont_eur_vs_asia|int}} times as many mapped novels as Asia, and North America {{cont_na_vs_africa|int}} times as many as Africa.

    Where novels are set, against where people live

    Share of novels with a setting in each continent (a novel can count for more than one) and share of the world's population (Natural Earth estimates). Hover a bar.

    The gaps are largest in the most populous countries. China has {{country.CHN.n|int}} mapped novels for 1.4 billion people, India {{country.IND.n|int}}, Pakistan {{undermapped.4.n|int}} and Bangladesh {{undermapped.0.n|int}}. The United Kingdom, with 67 million, has {{country.GBR.n|int}}. This is not a measure of how much fiction is written in those places. It reflects what has been catalogued in Wikidata, with English labels, by its mostly European and North American volunteers, and what gets translated and written about in English. {{countries_none_n10m|int}} countries of more than 10 million people have no mapped novel at all.

    07 · A map of WikidataWhy Denmark has more places than China and India

    Look closely at the map and Denmark glows. It holds {{bias.DNK.places|int}} distinct setting places, {{bias.DNK.places_pct|int}}% of every place in the data, more than any other country, including the United States ({{bias.USA.places|int}}). Denmark has {{bias.DNK.pop_pct|f2}}% of the world's people; China and India together, with more than a third, have {{bias.dnk_vs.chn_ind_places|int}} places between them. Danish works are recorded in extraordinary detail: the {{danish.works|int}} works in Danish have an average of {{bias_lang.6.per_work|f1}} settings each, against {{bias_lang.0.per_work|f1}} for works in English, down to individual Copenhagen streets. One Danish novel, {{danish.top_work|raw}}, lists {{danish.top_work_places|int}} places. {{danish.dnk_places_from_danish_pct|int}}% of Denmark's places come from works in Danish.

    All literary works. Red: the two countries where single cataloguing projects stand out. Hover a bar.

    Serbia is the second case. Its {{bias.SRB.places|int}} places put it eighth in the world, mostly from {{serbian.works|int}} works in Serbian, {{serbian.pre1930_pct|int}}% of them published before 1930, with an average of {{bias_lang.7.per_work|f1}} settings each. Both are the work of careful volunteers adding the settings of their national literatures, and both are good things. They are also a warning: a map like this measures the attention of the people who build the database as much as it measures literature. The same is true of Pittsburgh's place among the great fiction cities, and of the thinness of Asia and Africa. I have left the data as it is, and the novel counts used in the rest of this page are much less affected than the place counts: Denmark has {{country.DNK.n|int}} mapped novels and Serbia {{country.SRB.n|int}}.

    08 · Famous settingsThe best-known novel set in each country

    Wikidata counts how many language editions of Wikipedia have an article about each book, a rough measure of fame. The best-known novel on the map is {{most_linked.0.title|raw}}, with articles in {{most_linked.0.links|int}} languages; it is set in Spain. Next come {{most_linked.1.title|raw}} ({{most_linked.1.links|int}}), set in England, and {{most_linked.3.title|raw}} ({{most_linked.3.links|int}}), set in China. Choosing the best-known novel set at least partly in each country gives some surprises: wandering books win in many places, so Robinson Crusoe is the best-known novel set in Venezuela, and Paulo Coelho's The Alchemist in Egypt.

    The southernmost setting is the South Pole itself, reached in fiction by Jules Verne in 1869 and again in 1897. The remotest inhabited places in fiction are the islands of French Polynesia, more than {{remote.2.km|int}} km from any city of a million people, which owe their place largely to old travellers' tales and to Robert Louis Stevenson and Jack London. Wikidata also holds {{source.fictional_places|int}} invented places that it gives real coordinates: Lovecraft's Arkham in Massachusetts and R'lyeh in the South Pacific, Starfleet Academy in San Francisco, and 221B Baker Street. They are on the map, marked as fictional, but left out of every count of real places above.

    10 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. One Wikidata query, run with QLever in {{source.extract_year|raw}}, returned every item that is an instance of a kind of literary work and has a narrative location with coordinates: {{source.rows|int}} rows covering {{source.works_all|int}} works. Further queries gave each work's classes, genres, country of origin, language, form, sitelinks and English Wikipedia article, and each place's classes and country. {{source.works|int}} works have at least one setting on Earth; {{source.offworld_only|int}} more are set only on the Moon or Mars. A work counts as a novel if Wikidata gives its form as novel or novella, or if it belongs to a class with "novel" in its name (graphic novel, unfinished novel, light novel series): {{source.novels|int}} works. I made two exceptions: the {{source.decameron|int}} tales of the Decameron, which Wikidata files as novellas in the Decameron, and the Odyssey, whose form Wikidata gives as novel. Both stay in the literary works.

    Places. I sorted each place by its Wikidata classes into cities and towns ({{levels.places.city|int}}), single sites and parts of cities ({{levels.places.site|int}}), wider areas ({{levels.places.region|int}}), countries ({{levels.places.country|int}}) and continents, oceans and world regions ({{levels.places.large|int}}). Only the first three are drawn as points. {{levels.novels_country_only|int}} novels name only a country or larger area. City areas join every city or site within 25 km of a Natural Earth city of 250,000 or more people. A place's country is the present-day country its point falls in. The distance from home uses the coordinates of the author's place of birth, and "home" means the present-day country of that birthplace, which is not always the author's nationality.

    What this is not. This is a map of what Wikidata records, not of all literature. Coverage depends on volunteers, on English labels and on what has an article in Wikipedia; {{source.no_english_label|int}} works have no English label. Settings are often partial: a novel set in several places may list one of them, and some settings are recorded only as "New York" the state when the city is meant. Genres are as Wikidata gives them, grouped by me. Publication years are the earliest date recorded.
